WHAT (1) · Defining and recognising bullying

Workplace bullying can be defined as repeated and unreasonable behaviour directed toward a worker that creates a risk to health and safety (Kollerova et al., 2023). In a school context, bullying may involve aggressive interactions originating from students, parents, or professional colleagues within the educational environment (La Trobe University, 2019). Cyberbullying differs from face-to-face bullying because it uses digital mediums like social media, email, and online forums to harass and intimidate individuals outside of normal working hours. For teachers, workplace bullying may be particularly harmful because it attacks their professional confidence, destabilizes their classroom authority, and creates chronic psychological distress (AlBahsh, 2023).

WHAT (2) · Impact on teachers and school culture

Research suggests that teachers may experience bullying from students, parents, and administrative colleagues across their daily working lives. A significant concern for teacher wellbeing is the continuous erosion of emotional safety and professional efficacy caused by unchecked workplace aggression (Khairallah et al., 2023). Bullying by students, parents, and colleagues may affect teachers by inducing chronic anxiety, demoralization, emotional exhaustion, and severe mental fatigue (Ozkilic & Kartal, 2012). This is important because sustained occupational hostility is a primary driver of acute teacher burnout and premature career attrition across the sector (AITSL, 2020).

A similarity between workplace bullying and domestic violence behaviours is the presence of coercive control and systemic intimidation designed to strip the victim of autonomy and agency. Both may involve patterns of emotional humiliation, isolation, and power imbalances that trap the individual in a cycle of distress (Broderick, 2020). This comparison does not suggest the experiences are identical; rather, it highlights how similar power-and-control tactics create profound psychological entrapment for targeted teachers who feel they have no safe escape routes (AlBahsh, 2023).

One possible reason for the limited research is the intense professional stigma and fear of career reprisal that forces many educators to suffer in silence rather than report administrators or parents. A further explanation may be that institutional leaders often rebrand systemic bullying as individual performance management failures or poor personal resilience. Teachers may be reluctant to report bullying because they fear being labeled as incompetent or damaging their future employment prospects within insular school hierarchies (Zheng et al., 2024). Another reason this issue may remain hidden is that institutional protectionism often blocks external oversight, leaving incidents profoundly underreported and unexamined.

At an individual level, workplace bullying may completely shatter a teacher's coping mechanisms and psychological resilience (AlBahsh, 2023). The effects may extend to colleagues because systemic toxicity breeds a pervasive atmosphere of fear, silence, and distrust across the staff room (Kollerova et al., 2023). At a whole-school level, bullying can contribute to a fractured organizational culture and high staff turnover. Where a teacher is experiencing multiple forms of abuse, such as concurrent workplace bullying and domestic violence (Broderick, 2020), the impact may be compounded because the individual is stripped of safe harbors both inside and outside the workplace.

A published example illustrating this issue is documented in the news report regarding institutional retaliation after formally reporting workplace misconduct (ABC News, 2025). This case demonstrates how administrative structures often protect the institution rather than supporting the targeted employee. The example is significant because it shows how administrative failures directly compromise teacher safety and silence whistleblowers. This case can be linked to the research by Ozkilic and Kartal (2012), confirming that systemic bullying forces competent educators out of the profession.

Research suggests that teachers may experience bullying from students, parents, and administrative colleagues across their daily working lives. A significant concern for teacher wellbeing is the continuous erosion of emotional safety and professional efficacy caused by unchecked workplace aggression (Khairallah et al., 2023). Bullying by students, parents, and colleagues may affect teachers by inducing chronic anxiety, demoralization, and severe mental fatigue (Ozkilic & Kartal, 2012). Erikson’s psychosocial stages and Family Systems Theory help explain how intergenerational tensions manifest in schools, as younger students and parents clash with mature educators caught in generativity conflicts (Anderson, 2021). Within this relational matrix, Family Systems Theory demonstrates that anxiety and stress ripple across subsystems, making it difficult for institutional leaders to confront bullying when hierarchical power dynamics span across generations. Consequently, schools often struggle to address cross-generational aggression because administrators fear alienating vocal parent bodies or destabilizing traditional school-community hierarchies.

At an individual level, workplace bullying combined with external pressures may completely shatter a teacher's coping mechanisms and psychological resilience (AlBahsh, 2023). The effects may extend to colleagues because systemic toxicity breeds a pervasive atmosphere of fear, silence, and distrust across the staff room (Kollerova et al., 2023). At a whole-school level, bullying can contribute to a fractured organizational culture and high staff turnover (The Guardian, 2025). Where a teacher is experiencing multiple forms of abuse, such as concurrent workplace bullying and domestic violence (Broderick, 2020), the impact may be compounded because the individual is stripped of safe harbors both inside and outside the workplace.

A published example illustrating this issue is documented in investigative media reports detailing severe behavioral challenges and aggressive incidents involving students and families (The Guardian, 2026). This case demonstrates how unmitigated hostility directly threatens educator safety and operational stability. The example is significant because it shows how modern school environments fail to protect staff from escalating generational tensions. This case can be linked to the research by Kollerova et al. (2023), confirming that multi-source aggression severely damages staff morale and school culture.

SO WHAT

The PROSPER framework provides a way of understanding wellbeing through positive psychology, focusing on key domains like positivity, relationships, outcomes, strengths, purpose, and engagement (Noble & McGrath, 2015). Originally developed to enhance student mental health, it has proven highly effective in fostering resilience, academic motivation, and emotional flourishing within school environments. In relation to teacher wellbeing, PROSPER is useful because it shifts the institutional focus away from mere compliance and toward proactive, whole-school strategies that support educator professional efficacy (AITSL, 2020).

Maslow’s level of safety can be linked to physical and psychological protection in the workplace because teachers require stable, non-threatening environments to function effectively. Belonging connects with PROSPER because positive social connections and collegial support create a secure foundation for professional community and trust. Esteem is particularly relevant to teacher bullying because targeted harassment directly attacks an educator's professional competence, self-worth, and status within the school (AlBahsh, 2023).

When viewed through the PROSPER framework, bullying may undermine the positive relationships and engagement necessary for sustainable teaching. From Maslow’s perspective, this behaviour threatens the need for safety and esteem, leaving educators in a constant state of survival rather than growth. Together, these frameworks demonstrate that workplace aggression is not merely an interpersonal inconvenience, but a fundamental violation of human needs that destroys professional efficacy (Khairallah et al., 2023).

Repeated verbal abuse may affect professional efficacy because it steadily erodes an educator's classroom confidence and self-belief. Coercive behaviour can undermine a teacher’s sense of autonomy and control over their occupational environment. Cyberbullying may have a particularly strong impact because digital harassment penetrates private spaces, preventing teachers from escaping online abuse outside of school hours (Education Support, 2025). This may lead teachers to question their career choices and experience severe professional demoralization (Ozkilic & Kartal, 2012).

Workplace bullying may contribute to isolation when targeted individuals are ostracized by peers or unsupported by leadership during conflict. Professionally, a teacher may withdraw from collaborative planning, staff rooms, and school community events to avoid further hostility. The effects can extend beyond the school environment because chronic workplace stress spills over into domestic life, straining personal relationships and family support structures (Broderick, 2020). Over time, this isolation may trap the educator in a cycle of silence and helplessness, significantly increasing the risk of premature career exit (Zheng et al., 2024).

Family Systems Theory suggests that individuals operate within interconnected systems where stress in one subsystem ripples across the entire relational matrix (Anderson, 2021). Applied to a school, this means that an organization functions as an emotional ecosystem where staff, students, and leadership are mutually dependent. Bullying of one staff member may affect the wider system by creating an atmosphere of chronic anxiety, defensiveness, and guarded communication among colleagues. As trust deteriorates, the school’s relational culture may shift from collaborative collegiality to fragmented survivalism.

SO WHAT (2) · School policy and support

The selected Code of Conduct and policy recognises the importance of maintaining professional standards and mutual respect across the school community (AITSL, 2020). A strength of the policy is its explicit commitment to occupational health and safety standards for all educational staff. However, when considered through the PROSPER framework, the policy tends to focus narrowly on compliance rather than actively fostering positive staff wellbeing and relational resilience. One limitation of the policy is that it often treats bullying as an isolated incident rather than a systemic cultural issue involving students, parents, and colleagues. The policy gives limited attention to the psychological impacts of cyberbullying and online harassment. This is significant for teacher wellbeing because unchecked digital abuse leaves educators vulnerable outside traditional reporting channels. From a Family Systems Theory perspective, the policy fails to account for how administrative inaction can destabilize the entire staff relational network.

This case can be understood through PROSPER because the institutional retaliation documented in media reports completely shatters positive relationships and organizational trust (ABC News, 2025). Maslow’s hierarchy helps explain why targeted educators experience profound distress, as administrative failures directly threaten basic safety and esteem needs. From a Family Systems Theory perspective, the institutional response demonstrates how leadership defensiveness infects the wider relational system, silencing other potential whistleblowers. The example demonstrates the broader impact of bullying by revealing how administrative protectionism perpetuates toxic school cultures and drives competent educators out of the profession (The Guardian, 2025).

Activity 1 | Recognise and respond

  • Small groups analyse a fictional staff-room or parent-email scenario.
  • Identify bullying behaviours and map threats to Maslow’s safety and belonging needs.
  • Practise a respectful bystander response and a safe escalation route.
  • Theory: Maslow | PROSPER: Relationships, Resilience.
8 / 10

Activity 2 | Rebuild trust

  • Pairs use structured listening to explore how a reported incident affects a team.
  • Groups draw a relationship map showing how fear and silence can spread.
  • Agree on two practical norms for respectful collegial communication.
  • Theory: Family Systems Theory | PROSPER: Relationships, Positivity.
9 / 10

Activity 3 | Strengthen efficacy

  • Colleagues rehearse a supportive response to online harassment.
  • Use peer modelling, feedback and a short role-play to practise safe reporting.
  • Record one personal strength and one support contact.
  • Theory: Bandura social learning | PROSPER: Strengths, Engagement.
10 / 10

Actions and accountability

  • Immediate: clarify reporting contacts, non-retaliation and support pathways.
  • Immediate: make external wellbeing and leave information easy to find.
  • Progressive: review the school policy with staff, including parent and online abuse.
  • Progressive: repeat workshops and evaluate staff perceptions of safety.
